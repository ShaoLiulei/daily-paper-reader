Abstract 

Learning systems are typically optimized by minimizing loss or maximizing reward, implicitly assuming that improvements in these signals reflect progress toward the true data-generating process. However, in many practical settings—such as reinforcement learning with corrupted reward or learning from biased feedback—this assumption does not hold. In such cases, learning algorithms can converge stably while acquiring systematically incorrect solutions. This failure stems from a simple difficulty: when feedback reliability is unobservable (latent feedback reliability ), single-step feedback does not reveal whether an experience is informative or persistently biased. However, the situation changes when information is aggregated over learning trajecto-ries. Although individual observations remain ambiguous, trajectory-level statistics differ systematically across reliability regimes under persistent bias. We propose a general Monitor–Trust–Regulator (MTR) framework that infers reliability from learning trajectories and modulates updates through a slow-timescale trust variable. Across reinforcement learning and supervised learning, standard learning algorithms can exhibit stable optimization behavior while learning incorrect solutions under latent unreliability, whereas trust-modulated systems recover correct behavior and remain robust to persistent bias. In these settings, learning dynamics matter for more than optimization alone: they can also reveal whether feedback is reliable. 

Keywords: metacognitive regulation; epistemic identifiability; self-diagnosis; introspective learning; experience trust 

## 1 Introduction 

Learning systems are typically evaluated by their ability to minimize loss or maximize reward. Implicit in this paradigm is the assumption that sustained improvement in these signals reflects progress toward the true data-generating process. However, in many practical settings—such as reinforcement learning with corrupted reward, supervised learning with biased labels, or learning from human feedback [ 3]—this assumption does not hold. In such cases, learning systems can converge stably—exhibiting decreasing loss, shrinking updates, and increasing confidence—while systematically learning the wrong solution. In this work, we study this failure under latent feedback reliability : when the reliability of feedback is unobservable, instantaneous signals alone do not tell the learner whether experience 1

is reliable or persistently biased. When feedback is persistently biased, each observation can still look locally plausible, so no single-step statistic carries enough information to detect the bias. Standard learning algorithms—including SGD [ 13 ], Adam [ 8], and various robust or reweighting methods [ 12 , 7]—can therefore converge stably to a biased solution while exhibiting entirely normal optimization behavior. This can be viewed as an identifiability problem [ 14 , 2]: a parameter is identifiable if distinct values induce distinguishable distributions of observables; otherwise, even infinite data cannot uniquely determine the truth [ 10 ]. When feedback reliability is governed by unobservable latent variables, different reliability states can yield indistinguishable observations. The issue is not simply limited data or imperfect optimization; rather, the available feedback does not contain enough information to determine which regime the learner is in. To make this concrete, consider a minimal setting in which the learner receives a mixture of reliable and unreliable feedback but cannot distinguish between them. Standard updates can produce trajectories that look stable internally—loss decreases, updates proceed, confidence increases—yet fail to converge to the true parameter. Stable but incorrect convergence means that usual indicators of learning progress (e.g., decreasing loss or gradient norms) need not imply that the learned solution is correct. Faced with non-identifiability, common remedies use more data, stronger assumptions, or robust filtering; when the ambiguity is structural, such fixes hit a wall [ 14 , 2]. The bottleneck is information, not optimization: the system cannot know the truth from single-step feedback alone, yet must still act. If single observations do not reveal reliability, the relevant signal must lie elsewhere. Here we focus on learning trajectories: persistent bias may be invisible at one step, yet still leave regular patterns over time. This parallels metacognition in cognitive science—the ability to monitor and regulate one’s own learning [ 4, 11 ]. Metacognitive judgments often integrate evidence across observations on a slower timescale [ 5]. We adopt self-diagnosis : the learner maintains a slowly varying trust variable τ ∈ [0 , 1] , inferred from trajectory-level statistics, that modulates how strongly each update counts. Over time, this attenuates persistently misleading feedback while staying responsive when conditions improve. We formalize the idea in a Monitor–Trust–Regulator (MTR) framework with three parts: (i) extract stability-related signals from learning dynamics, (ii) integrate them into a slow trust estimate, and (iii) regulate parameter updates. Empirically, standard learners can converge stably to biased solutions under latent reliability, whereas trust-modulated learners recover correct behavior and remain robust under persistent bias. When single-step feedback is ambiguous, the learner may need to rely on patterns that only appear over longer stretches of training. The next question is whether trajectories contain enough information to recover reliability when single samples do not. To illustrate this phenomenon, we construct a minimal setting in which the failure is un-avoidable, as shown in Fig. 1. Under latent drift, learning dynamics can appear stable while converging to incorrect solutions, such that estimation error eventually increases with more data despite continued training (Fig. 1A). No local (finite-window) statistic separates reliable and biased regimes, so detectability stays near chance (AUC ≈ 0.5; Fig. 1B). Aggregating over full trajectories yields a different picture: features separate the regimes and support accurate detection of bias (AUC ≈ 0.85 ). As drift strength increases, detectability changes smoothly rather than flipping at a single threshold (Fig. 1C), so identifiability grows as more signal accumulates along the trajectory. The picture is asymmetric: failures can be locally unobservable yet still leave a signature at the trajectory level. We refer to this as scale-dependent identifiability under latent reliability.

Proposition 1 (Inevitable Misconvergence under Persistent Bias). Consider the one-dimensional quadratic objective 

L(θ) = 12 (θ − θ∗)2, (1) and suppose the learner observes biased gradients of the form 

g(θ) = ( θ − θ∗) + b, (2) where b̸ = 0 is a constant bias. Then any gradient-based update of the form 

θt+1 = θt − η g (θt), (3) with sufficiently small step size η > 0, converges to the unique fixed point 

θ† = θ∗ − b, (4) which is strictly different from the true optimum θ∗.Moreover, along this trajectory, the loss L(θt) decreases monotonically and the gradient magnitude ∥g(θt)∥ converges to zero, giving the appearance of successful optimization despite convergence to an incorrect solution. 

Proof sketch. The update defines a linear dynamical system: 

θt+1 − θ∗ = (1 − η)( θt − θ∗) − ηb. 

This recursion converges to the fixed point θ† = θ∗ − b. Since L(θ) is strongly convex, the iterates approach θ† with decreasing loss values and vanishing gradients. Thus, all standard indicators of convergence are satisfied, despite the systematic bias in the solution. As shown in Fig. 2, despite apparent learning progress—decreasing loss and vanishing gradients—the parameter converges to a biased solution θ†̸ = θ∗. Standard indicators of convergence therefore need not imply a correct solution when feedback reliability is latent.

is indistinguishable from the true gradient at each step. Stable convergence therefore does not tell us whether the learned solution is actually correct under latent reliability. ## 4 A General Solution: The Monitor–Trust–Regulator Framework 

The previous sections argue that when feedback reliability is unobservable, single observations are not enough, but trajectory-level statistics under persistent regimes can still separate regimes. A practical learner therefore needs to use temporal aggregates of its own dynamics, not only one-step feedback. The preceding analysis suggests that the problem is not solved by simply swapping optimizers. What is missing is a mechanism that estimates reliability from learning dynamics and uses that estimate to regulate updates. We formalize this as the Monitor–Trust–Regulator (MTR) 

framework. As illustrated in Fig. 5, a separable meta-level loop runs alongside the base learner: the Monitor extracts instability-related signals from learning dynamics, the Trust Estimator aggregates them into a slow reliability belief, and the Regulator modulates updates accordingly. 

4.1 Structural Requirements 

Before introducing the components, we make explicit the constraints implied by the preceding analysis: 

• No instantaneous signal: reliability cannot be inferred from single observations. 

• Temporal accumulation: useful signals emerge only over trajectories. 

• Persistent regimes: reliability manifests through consistent patterns over time. 

• Action under uncertainty: learning must proceed even without ground-truth reliability. Ignoring any one of these constraints makes the resulting learner brittle under latent reliability. In particular, methods that rely only on per-sample filtering, raw gradient magnitude, or static reweighting do not meet them.

4.3 Timescale Separation and Necessity 

The MTR design separates timescales between fast learning and slow reliability estimation. If trust tracked parameters step for step, it would mostly track noise; if it moved too slowly, regime changes would be missed. The framework therefore pairs fast dynamics that generate evidence with a slower track that interprets it. That split helps separate persistent bias from short spikes—harder to do when everything runs on one clock. This timescale separation shows up in the experiments. As in Fig. 6, τ moves more slowly than the parameters: it stays high under reliable feedback, drifts down under persistent corruption, and rises again when reliable conditions return. Even when individual outcomes do not label reliability, a slow aggregate over dynamics can track it well enough to regulate updates. Slow trust updates follow from Section 3: if reliability is only recoverable from trajectory-level statistics, a useful mechanism needs to integrate evidence over time instead of reacting to each observation in isolation. 

4.4 Discussion: MTR as a design pattern 

The Monitor, Trust Estimator, and Regulator hang together because of the constraints above: reliability is not visible per sample, informative structure appears along trajectories, and stable estimates need aggregation over time. A system that is meant to cope with latent reliability will implement some version of monitor–integrate–regulate, whether or not the components are spelled out this way. Accordingly, we treat MTR not as a single algorithmic instance, but as a general design pattern for learning under unobservable reliability. 

4.5 Mechanistic Illustration of Trust Dynamics 

To provide an intuitive visualization of the MTR mechanism, we consider a minimal bandit setting. This example is intended to illustrate how trajectory-level signals are integrated into a reliability estimate, rather than to establish the phenomenon. We consider a two-context, two-armed bandit task in which each context alternates between stable and volatile blocks. In stable blocks, the identity of the optimal arm changes rarely, whereas in volatile blocks it reverses frequently. Block identity and hazard rate are not observable to the 10 agent and must be inferred from the temporal structure of feedback. Individual outcomes stay locally ambiguous, which makes the task a compact illustration of learning under unobservable reliability. Within this setting, trajectory-level instability induced by repeated reversals is accumulated over time to form a slowly varying trust variable τ ∈ [0 , 1] . This variable operates on a slower timescale than individual updates and modulates the effective learning rate via αeff = τ · α.In the same spirit as Fig. 6, τ adapts on a slow timescale: persistent volatility drives gradual downregulation of the effective learning rate, while a return to stable feedback lets trust recover. This mirrors the logic of Theorem 2: single observations remain ambiguous, whereas trajecto-ries accumulate information that can distinguish regimes. The trust variable is the slow track that turns those path statistics into a concrete gain on the updates. This minimal example shows the mechanism more directly: learning dynamics provide evidence about reliability, that evidence is accumulated over time, and the resulting trust signal adjusts future updates. The same principle extends to high-dimensional learning systems, as we validate in the next section. 

## 5 Experiments: Learning under Latent Feedback Unreliability 

We evaluate how standard learning algorithms behave when feedback reliability is unobservable, and whether the proposed MTR framework can mitigate resulting failures. Specifically, we investigate three questions: (1) Failure under latent unreliability: can standard learning algorithms converge stably while learning incorrect solutions under persistent feedback bias? (2) Trajectory-level signal: do learning dynamics contain information about reliability that is not accessible from instantaneous feedback? (3) Effect of trust-modulated regulation: can trajectory-level inference improve robust-ness and enable recovery under persistent corruption? Our experiments are designed to expose these behaviors in controlled settings where feedback is systematically biased but locally indistinguishable from reliable signals. 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

We evaluate the proposed framework across two representative learning paradigms: reinforcement learning and supervised learning. In each setting, we construct environments with latent reliability, where feedback is corrupted by persistent bias that is not directly observable. To simulate latent reliability, we introduce staged regimes consisting of clean, corrupted, and recovery phases. In the corrupted phase, feedback is systematically biased while remaining locally plausible, ensuring that individual observations are indistinguishable from reliable ones. This setting reflects scenarios where feedback appears valid at each step but is globally misleading. We analyze learning behavior using three key quantities: 

• Trajectory instability St, capturing second-order statistics of parameter updates; 

• Trust variable τt, representing inferred reliability; 

• Recovery dynamics , measuring how quickly systems adapt when reliable feedback is restored. These quantities form the mechanism chain: 

St → τt → modulated updates → learning behavior .

11 5.2 Stable but Incorrect Convergence under Persistent Bias 

We first examine whether standard learning algorithms can exhibit stable yet incorrect learning under persistent feedback bias. Across all settings, standard learners show decreasing loss (or increasing reward), reduced update variance, and apparent convergence, while still learning systematically wrong solutions. None of this shows up as obvious instability in ordinary optimization metrics. As in Fig. 3, during corruption the standard learner (PPO) keeps training in a stable, non-divergent way yet fails to recover good performance afterward. Without divergence or wild oscillation, the policy can still sit far from where it should be. In other words, standard optimization diagnostics can remain well behaved even when the learned solution is wrong. Stable convergence here does not mean the solution is trustworthy. 

5.3 Trajectory-Level Signatures of Reliability 

We next investigate whether learning dynamics encode information about feedback reliability that is not accessible from instantaneous signals. While instantaneous gradients and loss values remain statistically indistinguishable across reliable and corrupted regimes, trajectory-level statistics reveal clear separability. In particular, we analyze the instability measure St, which captures second-order statistics of parameter updates over a sliding window. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the distribution of St differs systematically between regimes: under reliable feedback, instability remains low and concentrated, whereas under persistent corruption, instability increases and exhibits a broader distribution. These differences make the two regimes separable at the trajectory level. By contrast, local one-step signals remain close to chance-level detectability, as quantified for finite-window features in Fig. 1B. Taken together, these results show that reliability need not be visible in single-step feedback to become detectable at the trajectory level. 

5.4 Effect of Trust-Modulated Regulation 

We finally evaluate whether incorporating trajectory-level inference through trust-modulated regulation improves learning under latent feedback unreliability. Compared to standard learners, trust-modulated systems exhibit qualitatively different behavior under persistent corruption. As shown in Fig. 3, trust-modulated learning maintains recoverability: following the corruption phase, performance returns to near-clean levels, whereas standard methods remain trapped in degraded regimes. This improvement is accompanied by systematic changes in learning dynamics. The trust variable τ evolves on a slower timescale, as shown in Fig. 6, decreasing under persistent instability and recovering when stable conditions are restored. This adaptive modulation reduces the effective learning rate during unreliable phases, thereby limiting bias accumulation. These experiments indicate that using trajectory-level reliability estimates changes the learning dynamics in a qualitatively useful way: it limits drift under corruption and improves recovery once reliable feedback returns, compared with standard optimization-only training. 

5.5 Discussion: Why MTR-Like Mechanisms Arise 

The Monitor, Trust Estimator, and Regulator follow naturally from the constraints imposed by latent reliability. If reliability cannot be read off each observation, no amount of data or tuning of the base optimizer fixes that missing label—you need something else to look at. Trajectory-level statistics that build up over time are a natural candidate. 12 That yields two concrete requirements. First, pull informative quantities out of the learner’s own dynamics, not only from raw feedback. Second, smooth those quantities over a longer window than a single step, because one observation does not carry enough to infer reliability. Those two requirements push toward a split timescale: fast parameter updates and a slower reliability track. Without that split, the system tends either to chase noise or to react too slowly when the regime changes. MTR is one concrete way to implement this split. The details can vary, but the basic pattern is the same: monitor the learner’s dynamics, integrate those signals over time, and use the result to regulate updates. 

## 6 Discussion and Conclusion 

Stable optimization does not, by itself, guarantee that a learner is moving toward the correct solution. Under latent reliability, a system may reduce loss, stabilize its updates, and still converge to a wrong belief because the reliability of feedback is not observable at the level of individual samples. The usual shortcut—treating sustained improvement in the objective as evidence about the true data-generating process—then fails. The bottleneck is not always too little data or a weak optimizer; it is that single-step feedback does not say which regime the learner is in. Reliability can nonetheless show up in learning dynamics. Single observations may stay locally plausible, but their temporal structure can separate reliable from unreliable runs. That gives a practical handle where instantaneous signals offer none. This also changes how learning dynamics should be used in analysis. They are not just side effects of optimization; in these settings, they are one of the few places where reliability information becomes visible. Under imperfect feedback, good performance may depend on reading those signals as well as minimizing the loss. That matters for common deployment settings. Rewards are often delayed or shaped; labels can be systematically wrong in ways that single examples do not reveal; human feedback can be inconsistent or strategic. In such problems, trajectories may carry information that points do not. Systems that only trust one-step feedback in those environments are exposed to stable but incorrect convergence. The experiments here indicate that trajectory-level regulation can reduce that risk, though it is not a complete solution. 

Limitations and Future Work. We focus on settings with fairly persistent reliability regimes and on statistics computed over windows long enough to be informative. Rapidly switching or highly non-stationary reliability is harder and largely open. We also stress controlled benchmarks; large-scale and production pipelines need further study. Future work could use richer trajectory descriptors, probabilistic trust models, and tighter coupling with representation learning and large models. Understanding how learners can track the reliability of their own experience remains important for robust behavior in complex environments.

A.1 Environments and Protocol 

We evaluate the proposed mechanism in continuous-control reinforcement learning using Mu-JoCo environments [ 17 , 1]: Hopper-v4 and Walker2d-v4 . Each training run consists of 3M environment steps with 5 random seeds. Evaluation is performed every 10k steps using 5 episodes. Training is divided into three phases to simulate latent reliability: 

• Clean phase : reliable feedback (0–1M steps) 

• Corruption phase : unreliable feedback (1–2M steps) 

• Recovery phase : reliable feedback restored (2–3M steps) This staged protocol allows controlled evaluation of learning behavior under persistent reliability shifts. 

A.2 Reliability Corruption 

To simulate latent unreliability, we introduce structured corruption during the corruption phase. Three types are considered: 

• Advantage sign flip : flipping the sign of advantage estimates 

• Feature noise : adding stochastic perturbations to observations 

• Label flip : reversing target signals in value or policy updates For stochastic corruption, we evaluate corruption probabilities p ∈ { 0.3, 0.5, 0.7}.These perturbations are designed such that individual observations remain locally plausible while introducing systematic bias over time.

## Appendix B: Supervised Learning under Persistent Bias 

We further evaluate the proposed mechanism in a supervised learning setting with persistent structural bias. Experiments are conducted on CIFAR-10 [9] using ResNet-18 [6]. To introduce latent unreliability, we inject a consistent but imperceptible perturbation into all samples of a target class during the bias phase, creating a systematic gradient bias that is not detectable at the single-sample level. Training is divided into clean, bias, and recovery phases, analogous to the reinforcement learning setup. We compare SGD, Adam, and trust-modulated optimization. Results show that while Adam maintains stable performance during bias but fails to recover, and SGD eventually recovers but exhibits instability, trust-regulated learning achieves both stability and rapid recovery. The supervised experiments support the same picture: trajectory-based reliability cues help under persistent bias there as well, not only in RL.
